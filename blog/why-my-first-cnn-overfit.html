Why My First CNN Overfit

8 September 2026

What hitting 98% training accuracy and collapsing on validation taught me about shortcut learning, receptive fields, and inductive biases in vision models.

When training an image classification model for the first time, seeing the cross-entropy loss drop smoothly to near-zero feels like an unambiguous win. In my early experiments prototyping a custom convolutional neural network in PyTorch, the training accuracy climbed rapidly past 90%, eventually touching 98.4%.

Then I evaluated it on the validation split: 54.2% — barely better than random chance.

1. The Anatomy of Shortcut Learning

The intuitive reaction to overfitting is often to crank up weight decay or slap on an aggressive dropout layer. But diagnosing the feature representations revealed a more fundamental failure: the network wasn't learning the geometry of the target objects at all. It was learning spurious background correlations.

Deep neural networks are lazy function approximators. If a high-frequency background texture or a subtle lighting artifact reliably correlates with a target label across the training dataset, backpropagation will exploit that statistical shortcut long before discovering the invariant morphological features human vision relies on. 

# Diagnosing feature extraction: inspecting activation maps
def get_cam(model, feature_extractor, image_tensor):
    model.eval()
    features = feature_extractor(image_tensor)
    weights = model.fc.weight.data
    cam = torch.einsum('ck,chw->khw', weights, features)
    return cam.squeeze(0).cpu().numpy()

2. Receptive Fields and Spatial Priors

A key factor was receptive field size relative to feature scale. By stacking multiple large stride convolutions without proper residual bottlenecks, the early layers were decimating spatial resolution too quickly, forcing later dense layers to memorize localized pixel patterns rather than hierarchical abstractions (edges → textures → contours → parts).

Switching to smaller $3\times3$ kernels with batch normalization, progressive spatial downsampling, and balanced spatial dropout forced the network to preserve topological continuity.

3. What Biological Vision Does Differently

This failure was one of the turning points that drew me towards biological and organic-inspired approaches to artificial intelligence. Biological visual systems (from the retina through V1 and IT cortex) don't train on static, independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) image slices.

Instead, biological vision relies on:

  • Continuous temporal coherence: Objects move smoothly through time, providing natural unsupervised contrastive signals.
  • Local lateral inhibition: Cortical neurons actively suppress neighboring redundant activations, preventing dense memorization.
  • Foveated attention: High visual acuity is concentrated dynamically, rather than processing every peripheral pixel with uniform capacity.

Takeaways

A high training score without rigorous distribution-shift testing is an illusion. The real craft in machine learning lies upstream in data curation and inductive architectural choices, rather than simply fitting parameters until the training loss converges.
